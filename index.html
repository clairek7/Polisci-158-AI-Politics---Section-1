About me

I'm a Stanford undergraduate studying Economics and Public Policy. I'm drawn to the place where technology, public policy, education, and civic institutions meet, and to the question of who benefits when any of them changes.

I've worked on education-access projects, youth civic engagement, and efforts to make local government and community resources easier for people to use. Across all of it, I keep coming back to the same idea: data and technology can help us find problems that are difficult to see, and that's where better policy can start.

AI raises that question at a much larger scale. I want to understand how it works, and I also want to understand what it does to the people and institutions it reaches.

The technical side

How AI systems are built, what they can and can't do reliably, and how to measure whether they're working as claimed.

The human and political side

Who gets access, who gets represented, who is held accountable, and whether people can still trust the institutions around them.

Research interests

Six questions I want to keep working on, from democratic institutions to the information people rely on.

AI and democratic institutions

How AI changes political participation, representation, and public trust.

AI and public policy

How governments should use AI while balancing efficiency, accountability, transparency, and equity.

AI and education

How generative AI affects educational inequality, learning, access to information, and student opportunity.

AI and civic engagement

How AI could change the way citizens interact with governments and take part in local decision-making.

Algorithmic bias and equity

How algorithmic systems can reproduce or amplify existing inequalities.

AI, information, and democracy

Misinformation, political persuasion, synthetic media, and the changing information environment.

Project ideas

Six research concepts I'm considering for this course. Each one pairs a question with a way to test it.

Can AI make local government more accessible?

AI tools could help residents navigate city services, public meetings, policies, and accessibility resources. This project looks at how well they do that, and at the risks of excluding people or giving them inaccurate information.

Research question When residents use AI to find and understand city services, who is helped and who is left out or misinformed?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Audit of AI answers about real city services
  • City websites and meeting agendas
  • Resident interviews and usability testing
  • Accessibility standards (WCAG, ADA)

Does AI change who participates in politics?

Generative AI may lower barriers by helping people understand legislation, write public comments, or engage with government. It may also create new gaps between people who use AI and people who don't.

Research question Does generative AI widen or narrow the gap in who takes part in politics, and does it change what participants say?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Survey of AI use and political activity
  • Public comment records from agencies
  • Text analysis of comment style and similarity
  • Small field experiment with drafting help

AI and educational inequality

Students differ in their access to technology, guidance, and high-quality educational resources. This project asks whether generative AI helps close those gaps or makes them larger.

Research question Does generative AI reduce or widen educational gaps between students with different levels of access and support?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Student and teacher surveys
  • School-level data on devices and broadband
  • Comparison of AI use across school types
  • Existing education-access program data

Can AI detect political misinformation?

AI systems are increasingly asked to flag misleading political claims. This project tests how reliably they do it, and examines the risks of letting AI decide what citizens get to see.

Research question How accurate and consistent are AI systems at identifying misleading political claims, and what happens when they're wrong?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Fact-checked claim datasets
  • Testing several AI models on the same claims
  • Comparison across parties and topics
  • Review of platform moderation policies

AI-powered civic engagement

Imagine an AI assistant for local government that helps residents understand city policies, find relevant meetings, submit public comments, or locate community resources. This project designs one on paper and weighs the benefits against the democratic risks.

Research question What would a trustworthy AI assistant for local government need to do, and what safeguards would it need to protect democratic participation?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Case studies of existing civic tech tools
  • Interviews with city staff and residents
  • Design prototype and risk assessment
  • Public records and meeting minutes

Who gets represented in AI?

AI is now used to summarize public opinion and public comments. This project studies whether those summaries accurately reflect the range of views in a community, or overweight the voices that are already loudest and most visible online.

Research question Do AI-generated summaries of public opinion fairly represent different communities, and whose views are missing or distorted?

Possible methods and data sources

  • Public comment and town hall transcripts
  • Survey data as a benchmark
  • Comparing AI summaries with human coding
  • Demographic and geographic breakdowns
